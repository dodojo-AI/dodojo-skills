Claim 56/1214

Thursday, ten to eight. Two nights of frost have whitened every pane of the Hollin Winter Garden from the inside. Nothing has been touched since: Council's orders.

Calder & Wythe Mutual · Claim 56/1214

Hollin Winter Garden (tel. Hollin 40 office, 41 boiler house). Frost, night of 11th–12th December.

1. The Hollin Ghost£1,000

White cymbidium, Reg. No. 1887, in its original clay pot, cracked and bound with copper wire, number on the rim. Cover raised from £200 on 1 Nov at the Curator's request.

2. Palms and ferns£240

Received 12 Dec 1956

PC Dunmore blows on his fingers. “Inspector Hale wants your view by four. Stoker let her go out, if you ask me.”

The yard

Smoke rises from the boiler-house chimney. A handcart leans against the potting shed, H.W.G. stencilled on its side. Through the glass the palms hang black, like umbrellas left out in the rain. Down the hill the town's chimneys are lighting one by one. Dunmore stamps his boots on the cinders and waits.

The Palm House

Inside, the air smells of cold earth. Fronds droop from the iron ribs and frost furs the heating pipes along the floor. At the far end, by the orchid house door, a woman in a tweed coat stands very straight: Miriam Oake, the curator.

Dunmore lowers his voice. “Marsh waved me down off my beat at half six yesterday. She was here ten minutes after, wanting to go round on her own. I said not till the insurance had seen it. Nobody's touched a thing.”

Miss Oake

“Nearly seventy years,” she says. “It came from Burma in a sea captain's cabin, and one old man with a bottle let it freeze in a night.”

She locked up at six on Tuesday, she tells you, went home to her mother and stayed there until Mr Marsh rang at ten past six the next morning. “Ask Mother.” She takes a key from her coat. “The office. The valuations are on the desk. Leave the drawer; it's personal.”

Eight hundred pounds

“A London dealer, Mr Daventry, offered eight hundred pounds for it in October. I told him the Ghost belongs to Hollin.” A thin smile. “The Treasurer said that if it was worth that, we had better insure it for that. I put a little on top.”

She looks past you at the black palms. “He's still in town, at the Station Hotel. I expect he'd enjoy this.”

The orchid house

Smaller and closer, the panes whitewashed. On the centre bench a plant has slumped into black ribbons over the rim of its pot; the card in front reads THE HOLLIN GHOST, No. 1887. By the side door a drum turns under a glass lid, its pen scratching a line: the thermograph. Just inside the door a pipe comes through the wall with a red wheel on it.

The pot

The pot is light and smooth. Its rim is clean all the way round: no number, no crack, no copper wire. You tip it. Pressed into the clay underneath: BRANNOCK · HOLLIN MARKET · 7/6.

The roots that show at the drainage hole are short and pale. You think of the schedule clipped to your board, and you set the pot down very carefully.

The thermograph

The drum takes a week of paper. You find Tuesday evening: sixty-four degrees, steady as a ruled line, then one sharp notch, down and straight back, the way a door lets in a gust. After it, a long slide to frost.

Dunmore holds his torch over the notch. “What time did that door open?”

    Twenty to midnight

    “Twenty to twelve,” Dunmore says, and writes it down. Someone opened a door into this house at twenty to midnight on Tuesday. Five minutes later the line begins to fall, and it has never come back.

    The stoker's rounds, Dunmore says, are at ten and at two. Whatever stopped the heat stopped at a quarter to twelve, with somebody inside.

    The red wheel

    The pipe is as thick as your arm, and FLOW is painted on the iron above the wheel. You try it. It is screwed down hard: shut. On the wall side the pipe is hot from the boiler; beyond the wheel it is like ice.

    Dunmore whistles. “Marsh swears he never touches the house valves. His are in the boiler house, and they're open. I looked.”

    The boiler house

    Heat at last, and the smell of coke. Edwin Marsh, sixty-one, sleeves rolled, is feeding the firebox as if someone might take it away from him. A young man in a good scarf barrows coke from the heap: Tobias Venn, the assistant gardener. On the wall hangs a telephone, HOLLIN 41. Neither man looks up.

    Mr Marsh

    “Banked her at ten and went home. Back at two and she was roaring. Safety valve singing, mind; I took it for frost on the pipes. I've kept her going since, for all the good it does.” He stokes. “I've no key to the houses. Miss Oake has the lot, young Venn the front door since summer, me this one.”

    He wipes his face. “Aye, I had a drop at the Anchor. And come spring they put in an oil burner, and I'm finished anyway.”

    Tobias Venn

    “The Lifeboat Ball, till one. Ask anyone.” He rests the barrow. “I won the raffle. Twenty to twelve, on the stage, the Mayoress gave me a bottle of port in front of three hundred people.”

    As he pulls out the ticket stub, a card comes with it and drops on the cinders.

    L. Daventry · Orchids · Kew

    A place for a good grower. Write to me.

    Venn snatches it up, red to the ears. “He asked me in the summer. I haven't answered.”

    The office

    A lean-to off the Palm House, warm from the boiler wall. A desk with a blotter; a black telephone with HOLLIN 40 on its dial card; a board of hooks under a typed key register. The desk's one deep drawer is held by a brass combination padlock with four dials and a little tag on its shackle.

    The padlock

    Four brass dials, each from 0 to 9. The tag on the shackle is engraved with a single orchid flower and the letters No.

    Dunmore shifts his feet. “She did say leave the drawer.” You remind him that she has also claimed a thousand pounds.

      The drawer

      Station Hotel, Hollin · Telephone Hollin 9

      8th December. Dear Miss Oake, my offer of eight hundred pounds, in notes, stands until I leave on Thursday's 4.40. Telephone at any hour. I shall bring a warm box. — L. D.

      Borough Treasurer · 3 December 1956

      The Winter Garden will close on 31 March unless £600 is found for the boiler and glazing. The new oil-fired plant will not require a stoker.

      Under them lies the Ghost's old valuation, and nothing else.

      Harbour Street

      Down the hill the town is awake: milk floats, gulls, a smell of tar from the harbour. The telephone exchange is up the stairs over the post office. The Station Hotel faces the line, its brass plate polished bright: TELEPHONE HOLLIN 9. Under the market awnings the stalls are setting out.

      The exchange

      Six girls in headsets sit at a board of plugs and little lamps. The supervisor, Mrs Pell, keeps the night book under her elbow. “I can't read you the whole night, it's private. Give me a time, and I'll look at that page.”

      “Nothing else till six,” says Mrs Pell, and shuts the book on her finger.

      The Station Hotel

      The night porter, Albert Quill, already has his coat on. For a shilling he turns his book round on the desk.

      Night porter · Tuesday 11th

      10.30 pmMr Daventry (No. 6) up to bed.

      11.53 pmTelephone for Mr D. A lady. Would not give name.

      12.20 amMr D. out.

      12.50 amMr D. in, with a hatbox. Wanted his hot-water bottle filled.

      “Hot-water bottle for a hatbox,” says Quill. “Every night since, too. Takes all sorts.”

      Mr Daventry

      He is at breakfast in the lounge: a good grey suit, a kipper, the newspaper folded to the shipping. “Yes, I offered for the Ghost, and she refused me very finely. I am a dealer, not a burglar.” On Tuesday he was in bed by half past ten, he says, and slept like a stone. “My crates go with me on the 4.40.”

      A round hatbox sits on the chair beside him. Dunmore catches your eye: nobody opens a gentleman's luggage on a hunch.

      Brannock's stall

      Hyacinths in bowls, a tin bath of holly, and Mrs Brannock in fingerless gloves. She knows a cymbidium when she sells one. “Big unflowered plant, Monday. Seven and six. Nice leaves, nothing special.”

      Who bought it? She shrugs. “Scarf up to the eyes, paid in shillings. Wheeled it off up the hill in a handcart with letters on the side. H.W. something.”

      Your finding

      Dunmore opens his notebook on the cold lid of the handcart and licks his pencil. “The Inspector will want it short. Who let the Garden freeze?”

      Why?

      Dunmore writes the name and underlines it twice. “And why would they? That's the first thing he'll ask me.”

      Signed

      You read it back to him. Edwin MarshTobias VennMiriam OakeLucien Daventry let the Garden freeze on Tuesday night to claim a thousand pounds on a plant the frost would kill.to get Mr Marsh sacked and the Garden shut.to hide a swap: the Ghost was sold, and the frost killed the cheap plant left in its place.

      Dunmore buttons the notebook into his tunic. “Right. I'll ring the Inspector from the boiler house.”

      The 4.40

      Platform two, twenty past four. Daventry's crates are on the porter's barrow, the hatbox on top. Inspector Hale lifts the lid: felt, a lukewarm hot-water bottle, and the Ghost, leaves pale and whole, in a clay pot cracked across and bound with copper wire. 1887 is scratched on the rim.

      At the Garden, Miss Oake sits down on the potting bench. “Six hundred pounds,” she says. “That was all it needed.” The claim is refused, and the Ghost goes home on the handcart under Dunmore's coat.

      You named Miriam Oake and the swap. Clues found: 0 of 14. Hints taken: 0.

      Half a finding

      The claim is refused, and before dark Miss Oake is charged with trying to defraud Calder & Wythe. She says nothing at all. At twenty to five the London train pulls out with Mr Daventry in a corner seat and a round hatbox on the rack above his head.

      Your report said why she let the Garden freeze. It never asked what had frozen.

      What was missed: the plant on the bench was not the Ghost.

      You named Miriam Oake, for the wrong reason. Clues found: 0 of 14. Hints taken: 0.

      The wrong door

      Edwin Marsh is given his notice at noon, and Calder & Wythe pay the claim in full. He had no key to the houses, and the heat was shut off inside one.

      Tobias Venn is questioned until the Mayoress herself telephones to say where he was at twenty to twelve on Tuesday: on her stage, holding a bottle of port. Calder & Wythe pay the claim in full.

      Mr Daventry's solicitor is in Hollin by three. His client had no key to anything in the Garden, and at 11.53 he was taking a telephone call at the Station Hotel. Calder & Wythe pay the claim in full.

      On the 4.40, a round hatbox rides south.

      What was missed: who could be in the Garden office at 11.52 on Tuesday night, and who holds its key.
